Should I prune my hydrangeas now?

A fall haircut can eliminate next summer’s flowers on the wrong hydrangea. This decision accounts for the plant type, which wood it blooms on, local season and whether you want to shape or just remove dead stems.

Why this decision matters

If you cannot identify the hydrangea, do not cut healthy branches. Old-wood varieties can carry next year’s buds from late summer onward.

Old wood versus new wood

Bigleaf, mountain, oakleaf and climbing hydrangeas commonly flower from buds formed on older stems. Summer pruning immediately after flowering, before buds develop for the next year, is the safer shaping window. Cut those stems in fall or late winter and you may lose blossoms.

Panicle (including Limelight and Quick Fire) and smooth hydrangeas flower on new growth. They are generally better shaped in the dormant season, especially late winter or early spring. Removing dried heads in fall is optional; leaving them can add winter interest.

Reblooming and unknown varieties

Some reblooming cultivars such as Endless Summer bloom on both older and current-season stems, yet aggressive pruning can still sacrifice the first flowering flush. If you are not sure of the species, identification comes before a drastic haircut. In general, removing only definitely dead, damaged or diseased wood is safer than reducing live stems indiscriminately.
